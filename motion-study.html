Topic 1 · Overview

What is machinery?

A machine is a set of connected parts that takes in energy and motion in one form and delivers it in another, more useful form. The study of how those parts move, without worrying yet about the forces involved, is called the kinematics of machinery.

Inside every machine are mechanisms: assemblies of rigid parts connected so that moving one part produces a definite, predictable motion of the others. An engine turns the up-and-down motion of pistons into rotation; a windshield wiper turns a motor’s rotation into a back-and-forth sweep; a projector turns steady rotation into stop-start film movement. This tab shows seven of the classic mechanisms engineers use to do exactly these jobs.

Links

The rigid parts of a mechanism. One link is usually fixed to the frame (the ground link). The others are named by what they do: a crank rotates fully, a rocker swings back and forth, a coupler floats between two moving joints, and a slider moves in a straight line.

Joints (kinematic pairs)

The connections between links. Lower pairs have surface contact, like a pin (revolute) joint or a sliding (prismatic) joint. Higher pairs have point or line contact, like a cam touching its follower or a pin sliding in a slot.

Degrees of freedom

The number of inputs needed to fix the position of every link. For planar mechanisms, Grübler’s equation gives M = 3(n − 1) − 2j₁ − j₂, where n is the number of links, j₁ the lower pairs and j₂ the higher pairs. Most useful mechanisms have M = 1: one input drives everything.

Converting motion

Mechanisms change one kind of motion into another: rotation to straight-line motion (slider crank, Scotch yoke), rotation to rocking (four-bar), rotation passed between misaligned shafts (Oldham, universal joint), or steady rotation to timed or intermittent motion (cam, Geneva).

Topic 2 · Overview

What is fluid dynamics?

A fluid is any substance that flows and cannot resist a shearing force while at rest: liquids such as water and oil, and gases such as air. Fluid dynamics is the study of how fluids move and of the forces they exert on the surfaces they flow over and through.

It explains how a wing lifts an aircraft, why a long pipeline needs powerful pumps, how blood moves through arteries, and how wind loads a building. Engineers use it to design aircraft, cars, ships, turbines, pumps, piping systems and cooling systems. This tab looks at two classic cases: internal flow inside a pipe, and external flow around a wing.

Density and viscosity

Density ρ (kg/m³) is how much mass a fluid packs into a volume. Viscosity μ (Pa·s) is its internal friction, or resistance to flowing: honey is about 10,000 times more viscous than water.

Continuity

Mass is conserved, so the same amount of fluid must pass every section of a flow. For a liquid, A₁V₁ = A₂V₂: where the flow area narrows, the fluid speeds up.

Bernoulli’s principle

Along a streamline, p + ½ρV² + ρgh = constant. Where a fluid moves faster, its pressure is lower. This pressure difference is the main source of lift on a wing.

Reynolds number

Re = ρVD/μ compares inertia with viscous forces. Low Re gives smooth, layered laminar flow; high Re gives chaotic, mixing turbulent flow. It is the single most important number for predicting how a flow behaves.

Lift and drag

A body in a moving fluid feels a force. The part perpendicular to the flow is lift; the part along the flow is drag. Both grow with ½ρV² and are described by the lift and drag coefficients CL and CD.

How to read the simulations: red-orange marks fast flow and low pressure, teal marks slow flow and high pressure. Change the fluid, speed and size, and watch how the numbers and flow pattern respond.

Flow in a pipe: laminar vs turbulent

Fig. 8

The Reynolds number Re = ρVD/μ compares the fluid’s inertia with its viscosity. Below about 2,300, pipe flow is laminar: smooth layers slide past each other and the velocity profile is a parabola. Above about 4,000 it is turbulent: eddies mix the fluid across the pipe and flatten the profile. In between, the flow switches back and forth in turbulent puffs.

Topic 3 · Overview

What is heat transfer?

Heat is energy on the move because of a temperature difference. Left to itself, it always flows from hot to cold, never the other way. Heat transfer is the study of how that energy moves and how fast.

It decides how quickly a cup of coffee cools, why a pan handle stays safe to hold, how a phone keeps its chip from overheating and how much an air conditioner costs to run. This tab shows the three ways heat moves, then two machines built around it: an air conditioner, which pushes heat from cold to hot, and a power plant, which turns heat into electricity.

Heat and temperature

Temperature tells how hot something is; heat is the energy that flows because of a temperature difference. A bathtub of warm water holds far more heat than a cup of boiling water, even though the cup is hotter.

Conduction

Heat passes through a solid from particle to particle, with nothing moving as a whole. How fast it goes depends on the material’s thermal conductivity k:

q = kAΔT / L

Convection

A moving fluid carries heat away from a surface. Wind or a fan makes it much stronger, which is why blowing on hot food works:

q = hAΔT

Radiation

Every object gives off electromagnetic waves, mostly infrared, and they need no material to travel through. The hotter the object, the more it radiates, and the amount climbs steeply with temperature:

q = εσAT⁴

Engines and heat pumps

A power plant turns part of a flow of heat into work, but can never turn all of it. An air conditioner runs the idea backwards: it spends work to push heat from a cool room to the hot outdoors.

Air conditioner

Fig. 13

An air conditioner does not make cold; it moves heat. A refrigerant evaporates in the indoor unit and soaks up heat from the room. The compressor squeezes it until it is hotter than the outdoor air, and the outdoor unit releases that heat outside. The expansion valve then drops the pressure so the refrigerant turns cold again.

Steam power plant

Fig. 14

A steam power plant turns heat into electricity in a closed loop. Fuel heats water in the boiler until it becomes high-pressure steam. The steam spins a turbine that drives the generator, then the condenser turns it back into water using cooling water from the sea, a river or a cooling tower, and the pump sends the water back to the boiler.
