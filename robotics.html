What is a robot?

An industrial robot is a programmable machine that moves a tool or a part through space, accurately and repeatedly, without a person guiding it. Robots are the muscles of automation: they weld car bodies, assemble phones, pack food and carry goods around warehouses.

Every robot is a chain of links connected by joints and driven by motors. Its shape, called its kinematic structure, decides how it moves, how far it can reach, how fast it is and what jobs it suits. This page lets you drive five common types and then test your skills in a pick-and-place game.

Joints: R and P

A revolute joint (R) rotates, like an elbow. A prismatic joint (P) slides in a straight line, like a drawer. Robots are often described by their joint sequence: a SCARA is RRPR.

Degrees of freedom

The number of independent motions. Placing a tool at any position and in any orientation in 3D space needs 6: three for position (x, y, z) and three for orientation (roll, pitch, yaw).

Workspace

The volume the end effector can reach. Its shape follows the structure: a box for a Cartesian robot, a ring for a SCARA, a dome for a delta, a rough sphere for an articulated arm.

Forward and inverse kinematics

Forward kinematics: given the joint values, where is the tool? Inverse kinematics: given where we want the tool, what joint values put it there? The game at the end lets you try both.

End effector

The tool on the end of the robot: a gripper, suction cup, welding torch, paint gun or screwdriver. The robot’s whole job is to place it correctly.

Payload and repeatability

Payload is the mass a robot can carry. Repeatability is how closely it returns to the same point, often within ±0.02 mm on industrial arms.

6-DoF articulated robot arm

Fig. 1

The classic industrial robot: six rotating joints in series, like a human arm. The first three joints (base, shoulder, elbow) mainly set where the wrist is. The last three (wrist roll, pitch and roll) set which way the tool points. Watch the coloured axes on the gripper: they show the tool’s orientation.

Cartesian (gantry) robot

Fig. 2

Three straight-line axes at right angles, X, Y and Z, so each joint moves the tool along exactly one coordinate. That makes it the simplest robot to program and very rigid and accurate over large areas. 3D printers, CNC routers and many palletisers are Cartesian robots.

Delta (parallel) robot

Fig. 3

Three arms hang from a fixed base and join at one small platform. All the motors stay on the base, so the moving parts are very light, letting delta robots make several picks per second. Because the arms work in parallel, you choose where the platform goes and the controller solves for the three motor angles (inverse kinematics).

SCARA robot

Fig. 4

Selective Compliance Assembly Robot Arm. Two rotating joints swing the arm in a horizontal plane, a vertical shaft moves the tool up and down, and a final joint spins it. It is stiff vertically but slightly compliant sideways, ideal for pushing parts into holes on electronics assembly lines.

Drag to rotate · scroll to zoom

Forward kinematics: x = L₁cos θ₁ + L₂cos(θ₁ + θ₂), y = L₁sin θ₁ + L₂sin(θ₁ + θ₂), with L₁ = 500 mm and L₂ = 400 mm.

Wheeled mobile robot (differential drive)

Fig. 5

Two independently driven wheels and a free caster. Equal wheel speeds drive straight; unequal speeds turn; opposite speeds spin on the spot. The robot always turns around a point on the wheel axle line called the instantaneous centre of rotation (ICR, dashed).

Kinematics: v = (vR + vL)/2, ω = (vR − vL)/b, turning radius R = v/ω, with wheel separation b = 0.40 m.

Game: pick-and-place challenge

Game

You are the robot controller. Steer this SCARA arm (seen from above) to pick up each part and drop it in the bin of the same colour. Correct bin: +10 points. Wrong bin: −5. You have 60 seconds. Start with Joint mode to feel forward kinematics, then switch to Click mode, where the computer solves the inverse kinematics for you.

Mode

Keyboard: Q/A turn joint 1, W/S turn joint 2, Space grips or releases. In Click mode, click or tap where you want the gripper to go, then press Grip.

Game: drive a robot through the maze

Game 2

Guide a differential-drive robot from the start (bottom left) to the green goal (top right) as fast as you can, without bumping the walls. In Arcade mode you press forward and turn, and the computer mixes that into two wheel speeds. In Tank mode you drive each wheel yourself, exactly as a real differential-drive robot is controlled. Three range sensors show the distance to the nearest wall, like the ultrasonic or lidar sensors on real robots. Stuck? Autopilot plans the shortest route (breadth-first search) and steers along it with a go-to-goal controller.
